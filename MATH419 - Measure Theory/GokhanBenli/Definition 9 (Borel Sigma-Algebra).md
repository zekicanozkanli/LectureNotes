
> [!def] [[Definition 9 (Borel Sigma-Algebra)]]: The **Borel $\sigma$-algebra** of $\mathbb R^n$, denoted $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n)$, is the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the open subsets of $\mathbb R^n$.

> [!rem] Writing $\tau(\mathbb R)$ for the collection of open subsets of $\mathbb R$, we have $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)=\sigma(\tau(\mathbb R))$. Thus every open set and its closed complement belong to $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$, which is closed under countable unions and countable intersections. These operations apply to newly obtained sets as well as the original generators. The exact construction is $\mathcal B(\mathbb R)=\bigcap\{\mathcal A:\mathcal A\text{ is a }\sigma\text{-algebra on }\mathbb R,\ \tau(\mathbb R)\subseteq\mathcal A\}$, as in [[Definition 8 (Generated Sigma-Algebra)]].

###### Motivation.
As in a finite-set example, start with a generating collection and require closure under complements and countable unions; closure under countable intersections follows from De Morgan's laws. Instead of listing all Borel sets, express a particular set using these operations. The intersection construction gives the *smallest* $\sigma$-algebra containing all open sets.

###### Examples.
> [!eg] Ex. 1: The closed interval $[0,1]$ is a Borel set because it is a countable intersection of open intervals:
> $$
> [0,1]=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac1k,1+\frac1k\right).
> $$

> [!eg] Ex. 2: The half-open interval $[0,1)=[0,1]\cap(-1,1)$ is a Borel set. (see [[Exercise 6 (Half-Open Borel Interval)]]).

> [!eg] Ex. 3: Every singleton $\{x\}$, where $x\in\mathbb R$, is a Borel set because
> $$
> \{x\}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(x-\frac1k,x+\frac1k\right).
> $$

> [!eg] Ex. 4: The set of rational numbers is Borel because it is a countable union of Borel singletons:
> $$
> \mathbb Q=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb Q}\{q\}.
> $$
> Its complement $\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q$, the set of irrational numbers, is therefore also Borel.

> [!eg] Ex. 5: Open intervals with rational endpoints generate the Borel $\sigma$-algebra. Let $\mathcal G=\{(p,q):p,q\in\mathbb Q,\ p<q\}$. Then
> $$
> \mathcal B(\mathbb R)=\sigma(\mathcal G).
> $$
> Indeed, $\mathcal G\subseteq\tau(\mathbb R)$, so $\sigma(\mathcal G)\subseteq\mathcal B(\mathbb R)$. Conversely, every open set $O\subseteq\mathbb R$ satisfies
> $$
> O=\bigcup_{\substack{p,q\in\mathbb Q,\ p<q\\(p,q)\subseteq O}}(p,q).
> $$
> This union is countable because $\mathcal G$ is countable. Hence every open set belongs to $\sigma(\mathcal G)$, giving the reverse inclusion.

> [!eg] Ex. 6: Open balls generate $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n)$. (see [[Exercise 7 (Open Balls Generate Borel Sets)]]).

> [!eg] Ex. 7: Closed subsets generate $\mathcal B(\mathbb R^n)$. (see [[Exercise 8 (Closed Sets Generate Borel Sets)]]).
