> [!rem] In part (e), the stated function is a measure for $k>0$, but not for $k=0$. Indeed, enumerate $\mathbb Q=\{q_1,q_2,\ldots\}$. Then
>
> $$
> \mu_0(\mathbb Q)=\infty,
> \qquad
> \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_0(\{q_n\})=0,
> $$
>
> so countable additivity fails. The proof of (e) below uses the corrected condition $k>0$.

###### Proof.
> [!pf] *Proof.* **(a)** Let
>
> $$
> \mathcal A=\left\{\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\bigl((a_i,b_i]\cap\mathbb Q\bigr):-\infty\leq a_i<b_i\leq\infty\right\},
> $$
>
> where the empty union is allowed. Then $\varnothing\in\mathcal A$, and $\mathcal A$ is closed under finite unions: combining finitely many finite unions gives another finite union. All complements below are taken relative to $\mathbb Q$.
>
> For an elementary interval,
>
> $$
> \mathbb Q\setminus\bigl((a,b]\cap\mathbb Q\bigr)
> =\bigl((-\infty,a]\cap\mathbb Q\bigr)\cup\bigl((b,\infty)\cap\mathbb Q\bigr).
> $$
>
> If $a=-\infty$ or $b=\infty$, the corresponding piece is empty and can be omitted. Now take $A=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}((a_i,b_i]\cap\mathbb Q)\in\mathcal A$. By De Morgan's law,
>
> $$
> \mathbb Q\setminus A
> =\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\left[\bigl((-\infty,a_i]\cap\mathbb Q\bigr)\cup\bigl((b_i,\infty)\cap\mathbb Q\bigr)\right].
> $$
>
> Distributing these finite intersections over finite unions expresses the complement as a finite union of intersections of elementary intervals. Each such intersection is either empty or another elementary interval, since
>
> $$
> \bigl((a,b]\cap\mathbb Q\bigr)\cap\bigl((c,d]\cap\mathbb Q\bigr)
> =(\max\{a,c\},\min\{b,d\}]\cap\mathbb Q
> $$
>
> when $\max\{a,c\}<\min\{b,d\}$, and is empty otherwise. Consequently, $\mathbb Q\setminus A\in\mathcal A$. Thus $\mathcal A$ contains $\varnothing$ and is closed under complements and finite unions, so it is an algebra on $\mathbb Q$.$\blacksquare$

> [!pf] *Proof.* **(b)** Fix $q\in\mathbb Q$. For every $n\geq1$,
>
> $$
> (q-1/n,q]\cap\mathbb Q\in\mathcal A.
> $$
>
> Since $\sigma(\mathcal A)$ contains $\mathcal A$ and is closed under countable intersections,
>
> $$
> \{q\}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl((q-1/n,q]\cap\mathbb Q\bigr)\in\sigma(\mathcal A).
> $$
>
> Now let $S\subseteq\mathbb Q$. Because $\mathbb Q$ is countable, $S$ is at most countable, so
>
> $$
> S=\bigcup_{q\in S}\{q\}
> $$
>
> is an at most countable union of members of $\sigma(\mathcal A)$. Therefore $S\in\sigma(\mathcal A)$, proving $\mathcal P(\mathbb Q)\subseteq\sigma(\mathcal A)$. The reverse inclusion holds because $\sigma(\mathcal A)$ is a family of subsets of $\mathbb Q$. Hence $\sigma(\mathcal A)=\mathcal P(\mathbb Q)$.$\blacksquare$

> [!pf] *Proof.* **(d)** The only $A\in\mathcal A$ with $\mu(A)<\infty$ is $A=\varnothing$. Consequently, any countable union of sets in $\mathcal A$ having finite measure is empty and cannot cover $\mathbb Q$. Thus $\mu$ is not $\sigma$-finite.$\blacksquare$

> [!pf] *Proof.* **(e)** Fix $k>0$, and recall
>
> $$
> \mu_k(E)=
> \begin{cases}
> k|E|,&E\text{ is finite},\\
> \infty,&E\text{ is infinite}.
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Clearly $\mu_k(\varnothing)=0$. Let $(E_n)$ be pairwise disjoint subsets of $\mathbb Q$, and put $E=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_n$.
>
> If $E$ is finite, only finitely many $E_n$ are nonempty, and disjointness gives
>
> $$
> \mu_k(E)=k|E|=k\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|E_n|=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_k(E_n).
> $$
>
> If $E$ is infinite and some $E_n$ is infinite, both sides of the desired equality are $\infty$. Otherwise, every $E_n$ is finite, but
>
> $$
> \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|E_n|=\infty,
> $$
>
> since their disjoint union is infinite. Because $k>0$,
>
> $$
> \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_k(E_n)=k\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|E_n|=\infty=\mu_k(E).
> $$
>
> Hence $\mu_k$ is a measure on $\mathcal P(\mathbb Q)$.$\blacksquare$

> [!pf] *Proof.* **(f)** Fix $k>0$. We show that $\mu_k(A)=\mu(A)$ for every $A\in\mathcal A$. For $A=\varnothing$, both values are zero. If $A\ne\varnothing$, its finite-union representation contains an interval
>
> $$
> (a,b]\cap\mathbb Q,\qquad a<b.
> $$
>
> Every such interval contains infinitely many rational numbers. Thus $A$ is infinite, and $\mu_k(A)=\infty=\mu(A)$. Therefore
>
> $$
> \mu_k|_{\mathcal A}=\mu,
> $$
>
> so $\mu_k$ is a measure extending $\mu$. These extensions are distinct: for any $q\in\mathbb Q$, $\mu_k(\{q\})=k$. Thus different positive values of $k$ give different measure extensions of the same pre-measure.$\blacksquare$
