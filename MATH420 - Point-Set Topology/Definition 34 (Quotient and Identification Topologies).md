
> [!def] [Definition 34 (Quotient and Identification Topologies)](Definition%2034%20%28Quotient%20and%20Identification%20Topologies%29.md): Let $(X,\mathcal{T})$ be a topological space, let $E$ be an equivalence relation on $X$, and let $\pi:X\to X/E$ be the natural function. The finest topology on $X/E$ making $\pi$ continuous is the **quotient topology**. It is $\mathcal{V}=\{V\subseteq X/E\mid\pi^{-1}(V)\in\mathcal{T}\}$. Equivalently, start with a partition $\mathcal{P}$ of $X$, take its members as the points of $Y$, and let $\pi(x)$ be the member containing $x$. The finest topology on $Y$ making $\pi$ continuous is the **identification topology**, and $(Y,\mathcal{V})$ is an **identification space**. Again, $\mathcal{V}=\{V\subseteq Y\mid\pi^{-1}(V)\in\mathcal{T}\}$.

> [!rem] The two constructions agree because every partition determines an equivalence relation. The finest topology on the range making the natural function continuous is a **final topology**.

###### Motivation.
The identification space is obtained by *identifying each member of the partition to a single point*.

###### Examples.
> [!eg] Ex. 1: The five-point example gives a three-point quotient with topology $\{\emptyset,\{A\},\{A,B\},\{A,C\},\{A,B,C\}\}$, and its natural function is open (see [Exercise 97 (A Three-Point Quotient Topology)](Exercises/Exercise%2097%20%28A%20Three-Point%20Quotient%20Topology%29.md)).

> [!eg] Ex. 2: Identifying $(0,y)$ with $(3,y)$ in $[0,3]\times[0,1]$ gives a cylinder with its usual subspace topology (see [Exercise 95 (Rectangle Partition with Equal Heights)](Exercises/Exercise%2095%20%28Rectangle%20Partition%20with%20Equal%20Heights%29.md)).

> [!eg] Ex. 3: Identifying $(0,y)$ with $(3,1-y)$ gives a Möbius strip (see [Exercise 94 (Rectangle Partition with Reversed Heights)](Exercises/Exercise%2094%20%28Rectangle%20Partition%20with%20Reversed%20Heights%29.md)).

> [!eg] Ex. 4: Identifying opposite sides of the unit square at equal coordinates gives the torus $S^1\times S^1$ (see [Exercise 98 (Torus from the Unit Square)](Exercises/Exercise%2098%20%28Torus%20from%20the%20Unit%20Square%29.md)).

> [!eg] Ex. 5: Identifying antipodal points of $S^n$ gives real projective space $P^n$ (see [Definition 36 (Real Projective Space)](Definition%2036%20%28Real%20Projective%20Space%29.md)).

> [!eg] Ex. 6: If $x_n\to a$, then $[x_n]\to[a]$ in the quotient; the converse need not hold (see [Exercise 99 (Convergence under Quotient Projections)](Exercises/Exercise%2099%20%28Convergence%20under%20Quotient%20Projections%29.md)).

> [!eg] Ex. 7: Collapsing a subspace $Y$ to one point gives $X/Y$ (see [Definition 37 (Collapsing a Subspace)](Definition%2037%20%28Collapsing%20a%20Subspace%29.md)).

> [!eg] Ex. 8: Pairwise disjoint nonempty subsets can each be collapsed to one point, giving $X/Y_1,\ldots,Y_r$ (see [Definition 38 (Collapsing Disjoint Subsets)](Definition%2038%20%28Collapsing%20Disjoint%20Subsets%29.md)).

> [!eg] Ex. 9: The cone $CX$ is obtained by collapsing $X\times\{1\}$ in $X\times[0,1]$ (see [Definition 39 (Cones)](Definition%2039%20%28Cones%29.md)).

###### Proof.
> [!pf] *Proof.* Let $\mathcal{V}$ be the finest topology making $\pi$ continuous. If $V\in\mathcal{V}$, continuity gives $\pi^{-1}(V)\in\mathcal{T}$. Conversely, suppose $V\subseteq X/E$ and $\pi^{-1}(V)\in\mathcal{T}$. If $V\notin\mathcal{V}$, the class $\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{V}\cup\{V\}$ is a subbase for a topology strictly finer than $\mathcal{V}$. For every $S\in\mathcal{S}$, $\pi^{-1}(S)\in\mathcal{T}$, so $\pi$ is continuous into this finer topology by [Theorem 07 (Continuity via a Subbase)](Theorem%2007%20%28Continuity%20via%20a%20Subbase%29.md). This contradicts maximality. Hence $V\in\mathcal{V}$, proving $V\in\mathcal{V}$ if and only if $\pi^{-1}(V)\in\mathcal{T}$.$\blacksquare$
