> [!def] [[Definition 10 (Convergence Of A Sequence)]]: Let $(X,\mathcal T)$ be a topological space and $(x_n)$ be a sequence of points in $(X,\mathcal T)$. We say that $(x_n)$ converges to a point $x_0\in X$ iff, for every $U\in\mathcal T$ with $x_0\in U$, there exists $n_0\in\mathbb Z^+$ such that
> $$n\ge n_0\Rightarrow x_n\in U.$$
>
> That is, if $U$ contains almost all, meaning all except finitely many, terms of the sequence, then we say $(x_n)$ converges to $x_0$.
>
> We write $\lim_{n\to\infty}x_n=x_0$, or $\lim x_n=x_0$, or $x_n\to x_0$ as $n\to\infty$.

###### Examples.

i. Recall that if $X$ is an uncountable set, then the cocountable topology and the discrete topology on $X$ are different. But, as we can see from [[Exercises/Exercise 13 (Convergence In A Discrete Space)]] and [[Exercises/Exercise 14 (Convergence In A Cocountable Space)]], the collections of convergent sequences in these two spaces are the same. Hence we conclude that the collection of convergent sequences in a topological space is insufficient for describing the topology of that space.

Another example regarding the insufficiency of convergent sequences is related to continuous functions: see [[Theorem 3 (Continuous Functions Preserve Convergence)]].
